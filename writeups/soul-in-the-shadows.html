Soul in the Shadows LSB, base64 and a VORMIR XOR

forensics
flag OWASP{the_soul_was_here}

#1. The challenge

Soul in the Shadows There is more to this image than what you can see. Recover the hidden flag. Flag format: OWASP{...}

Attachment: a player archive containing a single image, archive.png, and a short challenge.txt repeating the prompt. The title and the "more than you can see" line both point at pixel-level steganography.

#2. Triage

First pass is always cheap metadata and structure inspection, because it tells you where not to dig.

Nothing exotic - a plain 8-bit RGB PNG, no alpha channel, non-interlaced. Next, walk the PNG chunk list and check for data smuggled outside the pixels:

  • Chunks present: IHDR, three tEXt, ~33 × IDAT, IEND.
  • No trailing bytes after IEND (a classic ZIP/append trick) - the file ends cleanly.
  • binwalk finds nothing but the PNG header and its own zlib IDAT stream - no embedded archive, no secondary file.

So the payload is not appended or embedded as a file; it must be inside the pixels or in the text chunks.

#3. The metadata hint

exiftool surfaces the three tEXt chunks:

Keyword Value
Title Forgotten Record
Author Archives
Comment Some things are left behind on Vormir.

The Comment is doing real work here. The title ("Forgotten Record") and author ("Archives") are pure atmosphere, but Vormir is a deliberate breadcrumb - in the MCU, Vormir is the planet of the Soul Stone, and the prompt is "Soul in the Shadows." A place-name like that is almost never scenery in a stego challenge; it is a candidate key, password, or XOR mask. Keep it in your pocket.

Interpreting the channel spec:

  • b1 - bit-plane 1 (the least-significant bit of each byte).
  • rgb - all three colour channels, in R→G→B order.
  • lsb - least-significant bit first.
  • xy - normal raster order (row by row, left to right).

So the message is stored one bit per colour-component LSB, a very common and very robust scheme. The surrounding prose ("checked twice", "nothing unusual") is flavour, and ::END:: is a self-imposed terminator. The middle line is the payload:

#6. Flag

#7. The three-layer chain

  1. Pixels - a base64 string hidden in the R/G/B LSBs (b1,rgb,lsb,xy).
  2. Encoding - Base64, hiding 24 raw bytes rather than ASCII.
  3. Cipher - repeating-key XOR with VORMIR, the key teased by the PNG metadata comment and the challenge title.

Each layer has a tell: the LSB channel produced readable prose, the Base64 string had the right alphabet and length, and the decoded bytes failed every text test (high-bit and control characters) - the universal sign that you are looking at ciphertext, not a mis-decode.

#9. Lessons / takeaways

  • Read the metadata first. A $0 exiftool call here supplied the XOR key. Metadata is where challenge authors hide hints they want you to find.
  • Titles and comments are content. "Soul", "Vormir", "shadows" were all signalling, not scenery.
  • Know what "not text" looks like. Control characters and bytes ≥ 0x80 after a Base64 decode mean "another layer", not "wrong channel".
  • Use known-plaintext. The fixed OWASP{ prefix instantly validates a guess at the XOR key; never brute-force blindly when the answer format is known.
  • zsteg -a is exhaustive here. Once it printed the ASCII channel, the bit-plane search was finished - no need to keep sweeping.

#Coverage / confidence

  • Complete - the zsteg -a sweep is exhaustive over bit-planes/orders, and the one payload channel is unambiguous and self-delimiting (::END::).
  • Key recovery is verified by known-plaintext (OWASP{ prefix).
  • HIGH confidence. Flag submitted once.
