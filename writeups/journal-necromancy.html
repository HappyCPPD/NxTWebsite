#1. The artifact

evidence.img (64 MiB, md5 5bdfa57396e9705f071904223f29d7f4) is a bare NTFS volume "WORK":

Two logical layers (both stamped 2026-09-30 00:20:18 - synthetic image):

  • Live: Documents/, Downloads/, Personal/{reminders.txt, vault/secure_archive.enc}, Photos/2026-03/IMG_2000..2005.png, Projects/.
  • Deleted: Cache/_c/ (~131 deleted c2_*/c1_*/c0_*.tmp, all 80 bytes), plus deleted wip.tmp, notes.txt, setup-2026-03.log.

Most .md/.txt/.csv files are corporate word-salad; the PNGs are solid-colour 8×8 images. All decoys.

#3. Finding the carriers - the magic marker

The decisive step: the three real carriers are tagged with a 4-byte magic de ad 53 47 (dead... - the "necromancy" theme). A single grep for it finds exactly three hits (the only other de ad runs are $UpCase-table noise, deac dead deae ...):

All three sit inside the binary tails of deleted files / a random cluster - the data clusters survive even though the files are deleted. (This is the whole puzzle: see §6.)

#4. The carrier layout - the one-byte trap

The reminder says "a 32-byte share plus a 1-byte index and a 1-byte xor-parity" but does not fix the order. Two interpretations both pass the parity self-check, because the parity byte is adjacent to the share either way:

Both satisfy parity == xor(share) ^ idx (so parity alone cannot disambiguate). The GCM tag disambiguates immediately: Layout Y verifies, Layout A does not.

#6. Why the "journal" angle is a red herring

  • $LogFile (inode 2) is genuinely 2 MiB of 0xFF (verified raw: icat 2 | uniq), i.e. the NTFS transaction journal was wiped. No $UsnJrnl / $Extend\$UsnJrnl exists either.
  • The title "Journal-Necromancy" and the wiped $LogFile bait you into journal recovery. The plaintext spells out the real lesson: "mft slack dies but data clusters remember" - deleting a file clears its MFT record/slack, but the file's data clusters are not zeroed, so the embedded dead5347 carriers survive and can be "resurrected."

#7. Pitfalls that cost the earlier attempt

  1. Parity is only a 1-byte check - scanning the whole image for [32 random][idx∈1..3][par] produces many coincidental hits in the two pure-random clusters (2155, 12133) and in the deleted cache blobs. Chasing those yielded three bogus "index-1" shares and a decoy idx-3 (c2_0017, which uses par==xor(share) without the ^idx term - a different convention). The dead5347 magic is the real discriminator.
  2. The byte-order trap (§4). Reading the share as the 32 bytes after the parity (Layout A) instead of before it (Layout Y) shifts every share by one byte and yields a key that decrypts to garbage. Always let the GCM tag arbitrate the layout - it is a perfect .
  3. NTFS record magic is the four bytes FILE (not FILE0); a 5-byte compare silently breaks hand-rolled MFT parsers.
