SPI TPM Free Trial Homemade TPM cipher, HNP lattice attack

Flag: hitcon{531f_dE5i6N_cRy9T0Gr4Ph1C_41G0r1ThM_15_9r0B4b1Y_4_64D_1D3a_QQ}

self design cryptographic algorithm is probably a bad idea - the flag says it all.

This is a reverse-engineering challenge that turns into a lattice (Hidden Number Problem) attack. The write-up is in two parts: (1) what the service actually is, and (2) the attack, including the one subtlety that makes the difference between "impossible" and "~5 minutes on a laptop."

#1. The target

The handout boots an aarch64 Linux VM (a patched qemu-system-aarch64 that implements three custom spi-tpm* devices) whose PID 1 is a stripped static-PIE service speaking a line protocol over serial. The service's crypto is delegated to a " firmware" shared object libspi_tpm_firmware.so - and crucially that .so is native x86-64 with symbols, so it can be dlopen'd directly on the host and driven with arbitrary inputs offline. All attack development is done against this firmware; the remote is only touched to capture one session.

#Protocol

Initial banner: budget 1024 queries 27. Every command (even errors) costs a query, and there is a hard 27-query cap per connection. Budget cost:

The flag is encrypted at boot under slot-1 with a large, fixed COST = 548787 and a random IV. We hold a chosen-plaintext under that same slot-1 key (encrypt 1 <cost> <hex>), which is what the whole attack rests on.

#The cipher

For each 32-bit plaintext word w:

  • perm is a TEA-variant and completely key-independent - its schedule comes only from (IV, block-index j, COST, selector). Because it's keyless, the local firmware can encrypt and decrypt remote blobs; we only ever need the key for the Feistel layer.
  • Feistel^COST is a balanced 16/16 Feistel over the 32-bit block, run COST times. One round: (L,R) -> (R ^ F0(L), L), with round selector r & 3 (period 4).
  • The only secret is the 128-bit key (kA, kB) (key bytes 0..15, full entropy from a real getrandom - no PRNG state to attack).

#Why you can't just decrypt the flag

The flag blob's nonce = COST = 548787 is baked into the header and authenticated by the MAC. A direct decrypt would cost 548787 * (ceil(70/4)+256) ≈ 1.5e8 >> 1024, and the nonce can't be lowered without forging the MAC. So the intended path is recover (kA,kB) and decrypt offline.

Dead ends (all measured, not assumed): the 4-round map has cycle length > 20M (no period shortcut); Feistel^a ∘ Feistel^b ≠ Feistel^(a+b) (the round counter resets per call); the key is a full 128 bits (no 32-bit reduction); SAT/SMT (, bitwuzla, CryptoMiniSat) all hang on the two 64-bit multiplies in sm, so inverting the round function algebraically from F0 samples is out.

An encrypt 1 1 <N words> (i.e. COST = 1, one Feistel round) gives us two very different leakage channels, both under the flag's key:

#(a) F0 samples - a perfect verifier, useless as a solver

For COST=1 the Feistel is a single round, and since perm is keyless we can peel it off:

So each ciphertext word yields one (L, F0(L)) pair - 16 output bits of the round function at a known 16-bit input, key-independent to extract. A single cheap encrypt gives hundreds of these. They can't be inverted to the key (that's the SAT wall), but they make a near-perfect filter: a wrong key matches ~182 independent 16-bit samples with probability 2^-2912. Zero false positives.

#(b) The key-check - a 9-bit leak of a bilinear form in the key

Byte 20 of every blob is

known_part(IV) is a public splitmix of GOLD ^ IV ^ CT (computable offline), and the public coefficients cA, cB, cC are IV-derived. So every blob leaks the top 9 bits of a known bilinear form in the 128-bit key. This is a Hidden Number Problem with a quadratic (kA*kB) cross term.

#3. The attack: HNP over the key-check

With n leaks we get n equations

Treat (kA, kB, z=kA*kB) as three hidden numbers (kA,kB are 64-bit; z is a ~127-bit nuisance). Eliminate z by pairing each equation with equation 0, build the standard HNP embedding lattice, /BKZ-reduce, and the key sits at a short lattice vector. Measured planted-vector norm, relative to the Gaussian heuristic of the lattice:

leaks n ratio ‖planted‖/GH status
26 2.48 wall - ~1e11 ball, infeasible
27 1.975 tractable - ~1.6e9 ball
28 1.58 easy (fpylll finds it in ~40 s)
29 1.28 trivial

The planted vector is not the shortest vector and not a basis row - BKZ won't surface it directly. You must enumerate the ball up to ~2·GH and filter. fpylll's evaluator stores every solution it finds (RAM blow-up at this count), so a streaming enumerator with an on-the-fly filter is required. The F0 samples from §2(a) are exactly that filter.

#The one subtlety that unlocks it: the 27th leak

A connection allows 26 encrypts plus one get_flag = 27 blobs. The natural reflex is "get_flag costs a query and returns the flag, not a leak → only 26 usable leaks," and at n = 26 the attack is a brick wall (ratio 2.48).

But the flag blob has its own key-check at offset 20, computed with the same slot-1 key and a fresh IV. It is a perfectly good 27th sample. Counting it moves the problem from n=26 (impossible) to n=27 (ratio 1.975) - the difference between the two columns above. The capture script in this repo had been discarding it:

Add it back (leak = key_check ^ known_part(IV)) and you have 27 leaks from a single connection, no re-capture needed.

#The enumerator

A naive Fincke - Pohst that scans [lo,hi] at every level spins forever at the top level (width ∝ √(R²/r_last)) on an LLL-only basis whose GSO profile spans ~1e15. Fixes:

  1. Schnorr - Euchner center-out ordering with early break - at each level start at the rounded center and walk outward, stopping a direction the moment the 1-D term pushes the partial norm past the radius.
  2. BKZ-40 preprocessing flattens the GSO profile (top level collapses to just 9 branches, x_last ∈ [-4,4]).
  3. F0 filter at the leaf: reconstruct (kA,kB) from the vector's first two coordinates, check a couple of F0 samples as a cheap pre-filter, then all 182; exit on first match.
  4. Shard the 9 top-level branches across cores.

Throughput ~3M nodes/s/core; the n=27 ball clears in a few minutes on 16 cores.

#4. Putting it together

Validation before trusting any of it: on locally-generated data with a known key, n=27 was solved in ~41 s and the recovered key matched exactly; n=28 was found too, confirming the enumerator's correctness.

#5. Takeaways

  • Count your oracles. The whole challenge is balanced so that 26 leaks are a wall and 27 just barely work. The 27th leak is hiding in plain sight inside the flag blob you were going to fetch anyway. Throwing away the key-check on the get_flag response is the difference between "unsolvable" and "five minutes."
  • A borderline lattice wants a streaming enumerator, not a bigger nr_solutions. The planted vector is near the edge of the ball; keeping the k shortest solutions never contains it. Stream the enumeration and filter with something that can't false-positive (here, 2912 bits of F0).
  • Keyless scaffolding is a gift. Because perm and the F0 extraction are key-independent, every bit of attack development - and the final decryption of the remote flag blob - runs on the local dlopen'd firmware with no budget spent.
  • And, per the flag: rolling your own ARX cipher and leaking 9 bits of a bilinear key-dependent checksum on every ciphertext is, in fact, probably a bad idea.
