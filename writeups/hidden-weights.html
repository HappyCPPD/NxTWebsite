hidden-weights orthogonal relation lattice

crypto
flag isfcr{0rth0g0n4l_l4tt1c3_1s_th3_tr1ck}

#1. The problem

chall.py picks n = 10 secret weights a_1..a_n uniformly in Z_M with M = nextprime(2^200), plus a secret m = 50 column 0/1 matrix X, and publishes only the 50 sums

Both the weights and the selection subsets are hidden. The flag is AES-GCM encrypted with

so we must recover the weights exactly as integers in [0, M).

The challenge text warns that "the usual lattice does not apply" - in ordinary subset-sum the weights are public and you search for the subset; here both are secret, so the classic (I | h) embedding is useless. The intended trick is the orthogonal (relation) lattice.

#2. Key observation

Let X be the n x m matrix whose column j is the indicator of the j-th hidden subset, so the published data is

For any integer vector c in Z^m,

If c lands in the integer kernel of X (X c == 0), the relation holds exactly, i.e. c . h == 0 (mod M). Because X is 0/1, ker(X) is a rank-(m - n) = 40 lattice packed with very short vectors:

  • h_19 == h_20 in the data, so e_19 - e_20 is a relation of norm sqrt(2) (the two columns of X are identical);
  • two columns whose integer sum coincides (e.g. x_a + x_b = x_c + x_d) give relations like e_a + e_b - e_c - e_d of norm 2.

A "generic" element of the relation lattice is long (~26 here), so lattice reduction cleanly separates ker(X) from everything else.

#3. The lattice

The full relation lattice

has rank m = 50 and covolume M. Since h_0 is invertible mod M (this is exactly why chall.py retries until gcd(h_0, M) == 1), a basis is immediate:

b_j . h = -h_j + h_j = 0 (mod M).

-reducing the resulting 50 x 50 basis yields a clean split:

Exactly m - n = 40 short vectors (norm² ≤ 9) - these span ker(X) - and n = 10 huge ones. The orthogonal complement of the short vectors is the row space W of X, of dimension n = 10.

#4. Recovering the secret 0/1 rows

W contains exactly the 10 rows of X as its only 0/1 vectors (the rows are dense random binary, so accidental 0/1 combinations are essentially impossible). Find them by solving the homogeneous 0/1 system

S has rank 40, so the solution space is 10-dimensional: take its RREF, pick the 10 free columns, and brute-force all 2^10 = 1024 0/1 assignments to the free coordinates, back-substituting the 40 pivot coordinates and keeping the integral 0/1 results. Output: the zero vector plus exactly 10 non-zero 0/1 vectors - the secret rows of X.

#5. Solve for the weights and decrypt

With X known the system is linear:

det(X X^T) is invertible mod M (checked). The recovered weights reproduce all 50 published sums exactly (X^T a == h mod M), which is a complete independent verification. Then

The flag name - orthogonal lattice is the trick - confirms the method.

#6. Reproduce

Runtime: a few seconds (one LLL of a 50-dim lattice, then 2^10 back-substitutions).

#7. Why it works (intuition)

With m = 50 > n = 10 the data is massively overdetermined, and the 0/1 constraint makes ker(X) a low-covolume lattice full of tiny vectors. LLL pulls those out first; their orthogonal complement pins down the row space of the hidden selection matrix, and the 0/1 points of that space are the secret matrix. Everything else (weights, key, plaintext) follows by linear algebra. No subset-search, no brute force over the 2^500 selection space.
